Seven Key Concepts

These seven concepts form the foundation for understanding how phishing and social engineering attacks work — and how to stop them.

1. Phishing

The use of fraudulent messages that impersonate a trusted entity to trick a victim into revealing credentials, clicking a malicious link, or opening an infected attachment. Phishing is a delivery mechanism for many different attacks, not a single attack type.

2. Spear Phishing

A highly targeted phishing attack directed at a specific person or role. Attackers research the target using public sources to craft a message that references real projects, colleagues, or events, which dramatically increases the success rate.

3. Business Email Compromise (BEC)

A category of attack in which the adversary impersonates an executive, vendor, or business partner to trick an employee into transferring funds or sharing sensitive data. BEC attacks often contain no malware and no malicious link, which makes them difficult for technical filters to detect.

4. Pretexting

The creation of a fabricated scenario — a pretext — that gives the attacker a plausible reason to ask for information. Pretexting is a core technique in both phishing emails and voice-based vishing calls.

5. Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)

An authentication method that requires two or more independent factors: something you know (password), something you have (phone, token), or something you are (biometric). MFA blocks the vast majority of credential-stuffing and password-reuse attacks.

6. Spoofing

Falsifying the source of a message or call so it appears to come from a trusted party. Email spoofing, domain spoofing, and caller-ID spoofing are all common. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC exist specifically to detect and block email spoofing.

7. Security Awareness Training

Ongoing education that teaches users to recognize, avoid, and report social engineering attempts. Effective programs are short, frequent, role-relevant, and paired with realistic simulations and positive reinforcement for reporting.
